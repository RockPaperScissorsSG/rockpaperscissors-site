Augmented Sailors, More Human Than Ever

A modern bridge is a noisy place. Radar calls out, AIS calls out, the cameras call out, and each instrument speaks for itself alone — which means the alarm that matters arrives in the same tone as the hundred that did not. Crews adapt the way anyone adapts to constant noise: they stop hearing it. Automated navigation has reached the point where its warnings work against the thing they were built to protect.

Toud Gward, based in Lorient and designed by sailors alongside the operators who use it, was built around that problem. Its software gathers what every instrument is seeing, reconciles it into one picture of the water, and then does the harder thing: it stays quiet. Only a confirmed threat reaches the crew.

When one does, it arrives through spatialised sound and haptics — the officer of the watch hears where the danger is and turns the right way before working out why. Proven in offshore racing, in search and rescue, and across six instrumented sites in the Molène archipelago under Project Morgane.

The technology opens the way. The sailor chooses the course.
